Managing Thoughts and Feelings

Thoughts, emotions and pain are closely connected. Understanding this connection can help you respond differently to pain and feel more in control day to day.

Understanding your experience

Living with persistent pain often brings a range of thoughts and feelings. These might include worry, frustration, fear, or low mood.

These responses are completely normal. Pain can affect many areas of your life, and the way you think about pain can influence how strongly it is felt.

How thoughts, emotions and pain are linked

Pain is influenced by many different factors, including thoughts, emotions, stress, sleep and activity levels.

You can think of pain like a recipe — different “ingredients” come together to shape your experience.

  • Worry and stress can increase pain intensity
  • Fear of pain can lead to avoiding activity
  • Low mood can affect energy and motivation
  • Unhelpful thoughts can make pain feel more overwhelming

Pain is real, even when it is complex

Persistent pain is always real, even if scans or tests do not show a clear cause.

Pain involves communication between the body and the brain, and sometimes this system can become over-sensitive.

Understanding this can help reduce fear and support you to try new ways of managing pain.

Recognising patterns

Pain can sometimes lead to repeating patterns where thoughts, emotions and behaviour reinforce each other.

For example, worrying about pain may lead to doing less, which can then affect mood, confidence and physical health.

Recognising these patterns is an important first step in making helpful changes.

What can help

  • Noticing and gently challenging unhelpful thoughts
  • Learning ways to manage stress and worry
  • Talking to others about your experience
  • Using relaxation or mindfulness techniques
  • Focusing on what matters to you, even on difficult days

A balanced approach

Many people living with persistent pain experience difficult thoughts and emotions at times.

This does not mean you are not coping or doing something wrong — it simply reflects how complex pain can be.

Learning to respond differently to thoughts and feelings can take time, but small changes can make a meaningful difference.
